Locus

Why this exists

Because "what happened right here?" is a question people ask while standing somewhere, and almost nothing answers it.

The question

You are in an unfamiliar town, or at a trailhead, or looking at a street you have walked past a hundred times. Something happened here. There is no obvious way to find out what. A search engine wants a name for the thing you do not know the name of. An archive wants you to already know which archive.

Locus takes the only thing you actually have — where you are — and answers with the historical records closest to it.

The sibling

This is the second applet built over the same open dataset. Circa locates you approximately in time: give it a life and it shows the history that surrounded it. A locus is a definite point in space, and that is the axis this one adds. Same history underneath, a different question asked of it, and deliberately a separate app rather than a tab bolted onto the first — the two have almost nothing in common but their data.

The dataset and the engine live in GeoHistory, which is open and answers to neither client in particular. Locus adds one narrow thing to it: a map, and an opinion about what makes a good answer to a point.

Why the privacy stance is not boilerplate

A precise coordinate is more sensitive than most of what people worry about handing over. It is where you are, right now, to within a few metres. Plenty of sites would collect that casually, put it in a URL so it lands in server logs and referrer headers, and keep it because storage is cheap.

Locus was built the other way around, and the constraint came first. There are no accounts, no cookies, no analytics, no saved searches, and no coordinate in the address bar. The pin exists in the page you are looking at and in one request body, and then it is gone. Privacy states that as a list of specific things the code does and does not do, which is the only version of such a claim worth reading.

Honest about the record

The history comes from Wikidata and Wikipedia, which are extraordinary and uneven. Dense cities are rich. Small towns need a wider circle. Genuinely rural areas need a much wider one, and what comes back is often a list of when nearby places were founded. Open ocean returns nothing.

None of that is hidden behind a spinner or dressed up as a result. Locus says which radius it used, says when it had to widen, and says plainly when there is little or nothing to show. A tool that answers "what happened near here?" with silence, honestly, is more useful than one that pads the list.

Getting more local history into the record is the part that matters most, because it is the part nobody else can tell you. That work happens upstream in GeoHistory rather than here.
